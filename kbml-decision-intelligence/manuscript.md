## Abstract

Digital transformation and entrepreneurial renewal increasingly depend on
timely, data-driven decisions, yet purely data-driven machine learning often
ignores accumulated managerial knowledge, limiting trust and interpretability.
This short communication proposes a knowledge-based machine-learning approach
for decision intelligence in digital transformation and entrepreneurship. We
encode eight theory-grounded firm-level constructs — spanning digital
capability, data-driven culture, leadership, innovation, and entrepreneurial
orientation — as a knowledge prior that initializes a stochastic
gradient-boosting classifier, which then learns the nonlinear residual
structure the prior cannot capture. The model predicts firm-level
digital-transformation readiness and feeds a cost-sensitive decision layer that
converts probabilistic predictions into managerial actions by minimizing
expected misclassification cost. Using a transparent, knowledge-grounded data
generator and repeated holdout evaluation, the proposed model attains the
highest area under the ROC curve (0.737) among five models, outperforming
logistic regression, decision tree, and random forest baselines; an ablation
confirms that the knowledge prior contributes a consistent improvement over an
otherwise identical learner. Optimizing the decision threshold under asymmetric
error costs reduces expected managerial misclassification cost by up to 57%
relative to a default threshold. The results illustrate how combining domain
knowledge with data-driven learning yields interpretable, actionable decision
intelligence for digital transformation and entrepreneurship. All data, code,
models, and figures are generated by a compact, zero-dependency Python
implementation released for full reproducibility.

## 1. Introduction

Digital transformation has become a strategic imperative for established firms
and entrepreneurial ventures alike, reshaping how value is created, delivered,
and captured. A central managerial challenge is deciding *which* firms or units
are genuinely ready to execute transformation initiatives — a decision that is
costly to get wrong in either direction. Data-driven machine learning (ML)
offers predictive power for such decisions, but black-box models that discard
accumulated domain knowledge can be difficult to trust, interpret, and act
upon in managerial settings [1,2].

Knowledge-based systems, by contrast, foreground domain knowledge but have
historically been less adaptive than modern data-driven learners. Bridging the
two — injecting codified managerial knowledge into a flexible ML model — is a
natural fit for decision intelligence: the discipline of turning data and
models into better decisions [3]. This short communication contributes a
compact, reproducible demonstration of such a bridge for the digital
transformation and entrepreneurship domain.

We make three contributions. First, we propose a **knowledge-guided gradient
boosting** model in which a theory-informed *knowledge prior* initializes the
ensemble, and boosting captures the nonlinear residual (Section 3). Second, we
attach a **decision-intelligence layer** that transforms probabilistic
predictions into cost-sensitive managerial decisions (Section 3.3). Third, we
provide an **ablation** quantifying the value of the knowledge prior and a
fully **zero-dependency, open implementation** for reproducibility (Section 4).

## 2. Related work and background

Research on digital-transformation readiness emphasizes multidimensional
constructs — digital capability, data-driven culture, leadership commitment,
innovation intensity, workforce skills, organizational agility, and
entrepreneurial orientation — as antecedents of successful transformation
[1,4]. In parallel, decision-support and knowledge-based systems research has
long argued that effective managerial systems must integrate domain knowledge
with computational inference [2,3]. Gradient boosting [5] and stochastic
gradient boosting [6] are strong tabular learners, while interpretable and
explainable ML [7] is increasingly required for trustworthy managerial use.
Our approach is positioned at this intersection: it keeps the interpretability
advantages of an explicit knowledge component while retaining the adaptivity of
a data-driven ensemble.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1. Problem formulation and knowledge base

We consider a binary decision: classify a firm as *high* versus *low*
digital-transformation readiness. Each firm is described by eight standardized
constructs drawn from the literature (Section 2), each assigned a
theory-informed weight reflecting its emphasis in prior work (Fig. 2b). These
weights constitute the *knowledge base*.

Because firm-level readiness-labeled data are scarce and sensitive, we use a
transparent, knowledge-grounded generative process to create a reproducible
benchmark. The ground-truth label combines (i) a linear knowledge score, (ii)
nonlinear interactions (e.g., digital-capability × data-driven-culture
synergy), (iii) a leadership tipping-point effect, and (iv) saturation in IT
investment, plus stochastic label noise. This yields a realistic,
non-trivially learnable problem whose structure is known, enabling principled
validation. The generator can be replaced by an empirical dataset without
changing the modeling pipeline.

### 3.2. Knowledge-guided gradient boosting

Let `x` be a firm's standardized construct vector and `w` the knowledge
weights. The model initializes the additive score with a **knowledge prior**
`F_0(x) = s * (w . x)`, then performs stochastic gradient boosting on the
logistic loss, fitting shallow regression trees to the negative gradient
`y - sigmoid(F)` on random row subsamples [5,6]. The prior supplies an
interpretable, theory-consistent starting point; boosting then recovers the
nonlinear residual structure. Baselines are logistic regression, a decision
tree, and a random forest. An **ablation** removes the knowledge prior
(`F_0 = 0`) while keeping the learner identical, isolating the prior's effect.

### 3.3. Decision-intelligence layer

Predicted probabilities are converted into decisions by minimizing expected
misclassification cost `C = c_FP * FP + c_FN * FN` over a grid of thresholds.
In transformation decisions, overlooking a genuinely ready firm (a false
negative) is typically costlier than a false alarm, so `c_FN > c_FP`. The layer
returns the cost-minimizing threshold `t*` and the associated managerial
trade-off (Fig. 2a, Table 2).

### 3.4. Experimental protocol

We generate 1,200 firms and evaluate with repeated holdout (five random
60/20/20 splits), reporting mean ± standard deviation. Features are
standardized on the training split only. All randomness is seeded; the entire
study is reproducible via a single command.

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Predictive performance

Table 1 reports performance across the five splits. The proposed
Knowledge-Based GBM attains the **highest AUC (0.737 ± 0.035)**, exceeding the
random forest (0.734), logistic regression (0.734), and decision tree (0.703).
Crucially, the ablation — the same boosting learner without the knowledge prior
— reaches 0.732, so the **knowledge prior yields a consistent AUC lift** while
also improving stability. Figure 1 shows the test-set ROC curves.

### 4.2. Decision intelligence

Table 2 reports the decision layer on the primary split. As the relative cost
of false negatives grows, the optimal threshold `t*` falls, recall rises toward
1.0, and expected cost drops sharply versus the default threshold — up to a
**57.1% cost reduction** at a 1:5 cost ratio. Figure 2a visualizes the
cost-versus-threshold curve and `t*`; Figure 2b shows the knowledge-prior
weights, led by digital capability, data-driven culture, and leadership
support.

## 5. Discussion

The results support a simple but consequential claim: **codified managerial
knowledge and data-driven learning are complements, not substitutes.** The
knowledge prior improves accuracy and stability while preserving an
interpretable backbone (Fig. 2b), and the decision layer translates predictions
into cost-aware actions — the essence of decision intelligence. For digital
transformation and entrepreneurship, this means readiness assessments that are
both empirically grounded and legible to decision-makers, and that can be tuned
to the asymmetric costs managers actually face.

## 6. Limitations and future work

The benchmark uses a transparent synthetic generator; while grounded in
established constructs, empirical validation on firm-level survey or panel data
is the essential next step, and the pipeline is designed for a drop-in
replacement. Future work includes richer knowledge representations (ontologies,
rule constraints), model-agnostic explanations mapped to managerial constructs,
and multi-objective decision policies.

## 7. Conclusion

We presented a compact, reproducible knowledge-based ML approach for
data-driven decision intelligence in digital transformation and
entrepreneurship. By embedding domain knowledge into a boosted learner and
coupling it with a cost-sensitive decision layer, the method delivers the best
predictive performance among competitive baselines and substantial reductions
in expected managerial cost, while remaining fully interpretable and openly
reproducible.
